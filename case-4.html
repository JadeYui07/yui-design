The Brief

A startup with a big mission
and an even bigger assumption

EdTechLive had the technology and the SEEDS survey methodology. What nobody had asked yet: why would a burned-out teacher add one more thing to their plate? Four months, six designers, one real question to answer before touching Figma.

Competitive Landscape

We mapped the EdTech ecosystem – and found two very different kinds of competition.

Competitors split cleanly into two camps: those using survey results to serve administrative and safety goals, and those adopting interactive activities as the data collection method. Neither camp was closing the loop with teachers.

Primary Findings

Finding 01
Survey data is used primarily for school safety and administrative measure — not for improving individual teaching.
Finding 02
Most non-survey solutions rely on integrated interactive quizzes or activities as the data collection method.
Key Insight
The biggest differentiator across the entire landscape is lesson-based tips and feedback — and almost nobody does it.
Finding 04
Every competitor maintains a separate "Educator Center" for professional development, siloed from the daily teaching workflow.
The Gap We Found
Nobody connects student data → personalized teacher tip → measurable change
Every competitor went survey → report. Full stop. Nobody closed the loop back to the teacher with something actionable for tomorrow's class.
Research

We talked to 8 people.
One answer changed everything.

8 interviews across educators and administrators. 5 with teachers. Online forums revealed what interviews often can't – newer teachers weren't struggling with curriculum. They were drowning in uncontextualized negative feedback with no idea what to do next.

This wasn't a data problem. It was a support problem. Teachers didn't need more surveys – they needed someone to translate results into one thing they could try tomorrow. Affinity mapping made it undeniable: administrators wanted monitoring; teachers wanted actionable guidance, fast.

Persona · Administrator
Monitor & Communicate
  • Track teacher performance across classes
  • Efficient pipelines for formal feedback
  • Increase student engagement school-wide
Persona · Educator
Advise & Improve
  • Fast, practical tips – not lengthy PD
  • Summarized, digestible data insights
  • Multiple periods create fragmented context
Research · Forums
Emotional Weight
  • Negative anonymous feedback is stressful without context
  • No tool helps teachers interpret student frustration
  • Trust is low without visible follow-through
The Call

The hardest UX decision is often
who not to design for.

Our research surfaced two user groups with fundamentally different needs. We couldn't serve both well in the time we had. Educators were the power users – touching the platform daily, with behavior change that would directly reach students. We designed for them first, deeply, knowing a strong educator layer would make the admin view meaningful later.

// Design Decision Log
"Based on all primary research, educators are more likely to use the software to summarize, update, and improve their course circumstances."
→ Unanimous team decision: focus on educator persona for depth
→ User journey scope narrowed to 4 stages: Onboarding → Collecting → Receiving Tips → Integrating
→ Admin views scoped to future iteration
Design Decisions

Four features. Each one
earned through research.

After 3 rounds of wireframes, we moved into a mid-fidelity prototype. Every feature below was shaped by specific pain points – not assumptions.

01
Modular Dashboard
A classroom is not a monolith. The dashboard shouldn't be either.

Teachers routinely teach 3–6 class types per day. We built a tabular class-switching system – multiple classes, no lost context, engagement data surfaced to the top.

Jakob's Law · Mental Model

Teachers already think in classes. Matching that pattern cuts cognitive load exactly when they're most pressed for time.

Dashboard · Tab System
Dashboard · Tab System
02
Calendar + Tip Requesting
Tips shouldn't live in a sidebar. They should live in the lesson plan.

We embedded "Request a Tip" directly into the calendar, linked to specific lessons. The client later promoted it to a primary CTA – validating we'd identified the right behavior.

Contextual Embedding · Zeigarnik Effect

Teachers plan lessons – that's the exact moment they need guidance. Pending tips create a loop that pulls them back.

Calendar · Tip Integration
Calendar · Tip Integration
03
Teacher Goals
Goals set in September shouldn't feel invisible in November.

We built a goals module – categorized by class environment and instructional strategies – that stays visible all year as a shared anchor between educator and administrator.

Goal-Gradient Effect · IA Chunking

Categorized domains make progress feel visible. When teachers can see the finish line, they keep moving toward it.

Teacher Goals · Category View
Teacher Goals · Category View
04
Student Inventory
Data about students that actually helps teachers teach.

Student learning styles visualized as a chart, linked to teacher goals. Small copy change: "UPDATE" → "RESEND" implied the survey had gone somewhere. Reduced confusion immediately in testing.

Recognition over Recall · Nielsen Heuristic #6

Teachers shouldn't memorize categories – they should see patterns at a glance. The goal link closes the loop from data to action.

User Testing

We thought we'd nailed it.
We were halfway there.

3 user tests on the mid-fidelity prototype. 6 tasks per session. Honest, occasionally uncomfortable feedback – exactly what we needed.

"The calendar layout is confusing. I feel more comfortable with a typical monthly view."
→ Jakob's Law violation · Layout redesigned to match existing mental model

Reflection

What I'd tell myself
on day one.

We shipped this. It went live. Every decision was traceable back to something a real person told us. The most valuable thing wasn't a tool – it was sitting with ambiguity long enough to find the right problem. The brief said "survey platform." Research said "teacher confidence." Those are different products. Scope is a design decision, and sequencing is design.

Lesson 01
The brief is never the problem
Research your way to the real question. The survey platform wasn't the product – teacher confidence was.
Lesson 02
Scope is a design decision
Choosing who to design for deeply, rather than everyone shallowly, is strategic – not a cop-out. Name it clearly.
Lesson 03
Testing surfaces what you're too close to see
We were proud of the custom calendar. Users found it confusing. Mid-fi testing saved weeks of rework.
Lesson 04
Client feedback is user research too
When the client pushed "Request Tip" to primary CTA, they were signaling observed behavior. Listen for it.

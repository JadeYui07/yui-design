Product Design · iOS · 2022–2024

Designing for
protective action,
not weather curiosity

The original product showed people weather information. It did not help them trust it, understand what it meant, or decide what to do next. I led the redesign around a clearer promise: help people act before severe weather becomes personal.

01 · The stakes

A product that lost trust
on three levels at once.

The app broke down at the moment it was supposed to be most useful.

01
Functional trust
The core features were hard to use

Hurricane tracking was buried among more than 35 interchangeable map layers. Smoke layers could render nothing at all. Wind showed color without communicating direction. The information was present; the meaning was not.

02
Reliability trust
It failed under pressure

Black screens on launch. A “Restore Purchases” button that triggered no visible result. A weather product that breaks during a storm is not a weather product.

03
Commercial trust
The commerce felt dishonest

Purchased ratings and a discount message that did not hold up to scrutiny. Users noticed, and said so in the only place available to them.

“I doubt the developer cares.”

— App Store review, pre-redesign

One sentence that summarized all three failures

Before improving individual screens, I needed to understand what the product should help people do when the stakes were high.

02 · The reframe

Severe weather was not a feature category. It was the reason people needed the product at all.

Design tension

The company was based in Florida, and hurricanes carried much of the product's value proposition. But hurricanes are seasonal and geographically concentrated. Someone in California, Kansas, or Minnesota needs a different reason to care — year-round.

Weather curiosity

“What is happening?”

Protective action

“What does this mean for me, and what should I do next?”

Design principle

Make the threat personal, make the information actionable, and make the source of truth visible.

03 · Three decisions

One mental model,
carried through the product.

01 ──
Make severe weather personal
The first screen had to answer: “Why should I care right now?”

Instead of opening with a generic welcome or a feature list, onboarding used the person's region to surface a severe-weather threat that was actually relevant to them: wildfire alerts in California, tornado watches in Oklahoma, hurricane warnings in Florida.

That turned setup into a chain of justified asks. Location permission was not an abstract request — it was what let the product show a threat that mattered. Notifications were not a generic opt-in — they were what made protection timely. Subscription stopped reading as a product decision and started reading as the last step of protecting yourself.

Relevant threat→Location permission
Location permission→Notification permission
Notification permission→Subscription
Constraint: the card had to be static rather than animated — every added megabyte affected download conversion. Copy, hierarchy, and imagery had to carry the emotional weight without motion.

02 ──
Turn awareness into a plan
The hurricane tracker became a planning tool

Competitors showed a static snapshot: where the storm is now. I designed the tracker around a more useful question — “If I leave tomorrow morning, what might conditions look like?”

A horizontal time scrubber moved across forecast waypoints while the cone, category, wind speed, and direction updated together, so time, location, and severity stayed in one model. Color-coded waypoints made intensity change scannable; the labels kept the explicit detail a high-stakes decision needs.

A static storm location→A time-based forecast
Isolated weather layers→One decision-oriented view
“Where is it?”→“What happens if I wait, or leave?”

Hurricane tracker · map layer → storm list → forecast detail. Selecting a waypoint isolates that moment in time: category, sustained wind, gusts and movement update together.

03 ──
Protect people and places, not only the current user
The unit of protection expanded from “me” to “people I care about”

Severe weather is experienced across households. Someone may live in Chicago while watching a storm move toward their parents in Florida. The Alerts experience split into two locations — Current Location and Favorite Location — with alerts grouped by city so it stayed obvious which place needed attention.

The alert detail view embedded the official NHC Seven-Day Graphical Tropical Weather Outlook rather than replacing it with an opaque interpretation. People in high-stakes situations saw the same chart emergency managers see.

Design principle

In a high-stakes context, trust comes from making the evidence inspectable — not only from making the interface simpler.

A saved location, in its own context. The active watch banner sits above the map for the place you are watching — not only the place you are standing in.

Supporting decision · Discovery

The same promise continued
into the App Store.

The hurricane-season App Store promotion used the product's own storm cones and paths, with copy written like a weather alert — urgency, specificity, and action rather than generic weather imagery. The same visual and verbal language that ran through the product ran through the top of the funnel.

App Store live events, shipped storm by storm — Idalia, Harold, Hilary — each running inside that storm’s own window.

The promotion window was associated with an approximately 30% increase in downloads.

05 · What I learned

The most important decision was
choosing the right mental model.

The strongest work here was not a component or a screen. It was deciding that the product should help people act, not simply help them observe weather. Once that was settled, the rest of the decisions stopped competing with each other:

  • Onboarding made the threat personally relevant.
  • The tracker connected forecast changes to planning.
  • Alerts expanded protection to the people and places users cared about.
  • The source of critical information stayed visible.

When the stakes are high, a product earns trust by helping people understand what matters, why it matters, and what they can do next.
